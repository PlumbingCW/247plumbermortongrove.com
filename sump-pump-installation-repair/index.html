Emergency Sump Pump Repair and Installation in Morton Grove, IL

A pump that stops during a storm lets the pit overflow, so the fix is to restore the pump and its power fast.

24/7 Emergency Line Open
Call (847) 281-8626

A sump pump is a motorized pump in a pit that moves groundwater out of a basement before it reaches the floor. When it quits during a storm, water can cover a basement floor in under an hour. Emergency sump pump repair in Morton Grove, IL starts with a call to licensed plumbers who are dispatched 24/7, including nights and weekends.

While you wait, keep everyone out of standing water and stay away from the panel and outlets if they are wet. Lift the pit lid and look at the water level. A full pit with a silent pump tells the plumber a lot before the truck arrives.

What a failing sump pump looks like

  • The pit is full or overflowing and the pump makes no sound
  • The motor hums but no water leaves through the discharge pipe
  • The pump runs nonstop, even when it has not rained
  • The float arm is jammed against the pit wall or stuck high
  • The pump cycles on and off every few seconds
  • Water sits at the basement floor edge and a musty smell is growing
  • The breaker trips whenever the pump starts

When a failed sump pump is an emergency

Not every noisy pump is a crisis. A pump that rattles but keeps the pit empty can wait for a same-day appointment when available. It becomes an emergency when water is rising in the pit, the pump will not start, or water has already reached the floor.

Rain is the usual trigger, and the power often fails in the same storm. That is why a dead pump and a dark house tend to arrive together. If you are unsure which situation you have, call. The dispatcher can sort it out in a minute or two, at any hour.

What to do while you wait

  • Stay out of the basement if water is near outlets, the panel or any appliance plugged in below
  • Kill the basement circuit at the panel only if you can walk to it on a dry floor, with no puddle in between
  • Never unplug a pump while standing in water
  • Move boxes, rugs and stored items up off the floor if it is safe to do so
  • Do not run the washing machine or dishwasher, because that adds water to the same system
  • Keep the pit lid closed unless you are checking the water level

How a plumber finds out why it quit

Most pump failures come down to a short list of causes. The plumber checks power first, then the float, then the check valve and discharge line, and finally the motor itself. Running the pump with a bucket of water often shows the problem in seconds.

What the plumber sees Likely cause Usual next step
No sound, pit full Tripped breaker, dead outlet or burned-out motor Reset and test power, then replace pump if the motor is dead
Hum but no water leaving Stuck impeller or clogged intake Clear debris, or replace the pump
Runs without stopping Float stuck, or check valve failing Free or replace the float, then test the valve
Water flows back after each cycle Missing or failed check valve Install a new check valve
Rapid on and off cycling Pit too small or float set too tight Reset float height, or fit a larger pit liner

Repair or replace the pump

A float switch or check valve is a small repair and usually makes sense to fix. A burned motor or a pump that has run for many seasons is a different story. Replacing it costs more up front, but you stop paying for repeat visits.

A pedestal pump sits on top of the pit and is easier to service. A submersible pump sits inside the water, runs quieter and handles heavier loads. The plumber should explain which one suits your pit and why, and give a clear price before work starts.

Battery backup and power loss

A standard pump stops the moment the electricity does. A battery backup is a second pump mounted next to the primary one. If the primary dies or the lights go out, the backup keeps moving water on stored charge. That charge runs down, so ask how many hours of pumping the battery gives under a heavy inflow.

Water-powered backups use household water pressure instead of a battery. They need no charging, but they only work where the pressure is steady and the drain connection is correct. A plumber can walk you through which type fits your basement. Either way, test the backup a few times a year by pouring water into the pit.

Where the discharge water is allowed to go

Sump water must never be piped into the sanitary sewer. Under Morton Grove Municipal Code 7-5-4A, the sanitary sewer is off limits for roof downspouts, footing drains, and the drains in driveways or stairwells. That water belongs in a separate storm system, and a sump pit with a pump counts as one.

This matters in a heavy storm. Clean water sent into a sanitary line adds to the same pipes that back up into basements. If your discharge line ties into a floor drain or a sewer cleanout, tell the plumber. The fix is to reroute it outdoors. A permit question may apply, so call Morton Grove's building department on (847) 470-5214 before anything gets moved.

Ejector pumps for basement bathrooms

A sump pump handles groundwater. An ejector pump is a separate unit that lifts wastewater from a basement toilet, sink or shower up to the main sewer line. It sits in a sealed basin and needs its own vent, check valve and power. If the basin alarm sounds or a basement drain gurgles, treat it as sewage and stay out of contact. For a main line that backs up repeatedly, see our sewer line repair page.

Pump trouble or something else

Sometimes the pump works and the basement still gets wet. Water that appears along one wall may come from a hidden supply leak, which a leak detection visit can find with acoustic listening. Seepage through the floor-wall joint over many storms points to drainage problems that basement waterproofing can address.

What changes the price of sump pump work

  • Repair or replace: a new float or check valve costs far less than a full pump and pit
  • Pump type and power: submersible units and higher horsepower pumps cost more
  • Backup system: adding a battery or water-powered backup raises the total
  • Pit and plumbing work: a new liner, a longer discharge run or rerouting away from the sewer adds labor
  • Access and timing: tight basements and after-hours calls change the time on site
