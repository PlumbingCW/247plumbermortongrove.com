Basement Waterproofing in Morton Grove, IL

Dry basements come from moving water away from the walls and floor, not from painting over the problem.

Call (847) 281-8626

Basement waterproofing is the set of drainage, sealing and pumping steps that keep groundwater and surface water out of a below-grade space. In Morton Grove, IL, the right plan depends on where the water enters, how often, and whether it comes from the ground or from the sewer. Licensed plumbers can sort out which one you have before any work is priced.

Most homes here are owner-occupied, with 85.2% homeownership in 2024, so a wet basement is usually the owner's problem to solve and to pay for. That makes a clear diagnosis worth more than a quick patch.

Signs your basement needs a waterproofing look

  • Damp patches or white mineral crust on the walls
  • Water at the floor-wall joint after heavy rain
  • A musty smell that returns even after cleaning
  • Cracks in the floor or walls that darken or leak
  • Water bubbling up through a floor drain
  • Peeling paint or blistering sealer on masonry
  • Carpet edges or stored boxes that stay damp

First, find out where the water comes from

Water in a basement has four common routes. It can seep through the walls, rise through the floor slab, come in through cracks, or back up through a drain. Each needs a different answer, and a repair for one will not stop another.

A plumber starts with a walk-through and a moisture check. A leak detection visit helps rule out a supply or drain pipe that is leaking inside the wall, which looks like groundwater but is not.

Sealants versus drainage

Surface sealers and masonry paints block a thin film of moisture. They do not stop water pushing in under pressure, and they tend to peel when the wall stays wet behind them. Sealants have a place for small, dry-weather dampness and for patching a single crack.

Drainage solves a different problem. It gives water a path to a pit and a pump so it never builds up against the wall. If water appears after storms and not in dry spells, drainage is usually the answer and sealer alone is usually a waste.

Interior drain systems

An interior drain runs along the inside edge of the footing, beneath the slab. It catches water at the floor-wall joint and carries it to a sump pit. The work involves cutting a channel in the floor, laying perforated pipe in gravel, and patching the concrete.

This approach is cleaner than digging outside and works in winter. It does not stop water from touching the outside of the wall, so a damp wall can remain. For the pit that ends the line, see sump pump repair and installation.

Floor drains and the sewer side

Not every wet basement is a groundwater problem. A floor drain connects to the house drain, and in a heavy storm that line can back up. Sewer water is dirty, so avoid contact and do not treat it like a seepage fix.

If your drain backs up repeatedly, a camera inspection of the lateral can show a belly, a root intrusion or a collapse. A broken line is a job for sewer line repair, not for drain tile.

Cracks, joints and what actually stops them

Hairline cracks in poured concrete are common and often harmless if they stay dry. A crack that weeps after rain is a different matter. Plumbers use injected polyurethane or epoxy to seal a leaking crack from the inside, which works well for one active crack.

If cracks keep opening or widen over time, the wall may be moving. That is a structural question, so ask a structural engineer before you pay for more sealing. A good waterproofing contractor will tell you when the problem is not theirs.

Backup prevention is a different tool

Waterproofing keeps groundwater out. It does nothing for sewage that comes back up through a basement drain. Morton Grove has a village flood control assistance program aimed at that exact problem. It pays toward changing how your house drains to the sewer so wastewater can no longer flow backward into the basement.

Two fixes qualify. One is an overhead sewer, which lifts household wastewater to a pump. The other is a backflow valve outside the house, paired with a bypass pump in a buried vault. The village loan pays half of the actual cost, capped at $5,000.

It is limited to owner-occupied homes built before 1965, once per address. You must collect estimates in writing from three separate contractors who hold Illinois licences. Funding is not promised from one year to the next, so call Public Works at (847) 470-5235 and ask whether the program is open.

Sump pumps, battery backup and discharge

A pit and pump are the heart of most interior systems. The pump should discharge to a separate storm system, since the village code bars footing drains and downspouts from the sanitary sewer. A battery or water-powered backup keeps the system running when storms knock out power.

Ask any contractor how big the pit is, how fast the pump moves water, and what happens in a power failure. If the answers are vague, get another quote. We work in Skokie and nearby, where the same questions apply.

What changes the price of basement waterproofing

  • Method: an interior drain costs less than a full exterior dig on the same wall
  • Length of wall and floor involved: more linear feet means more labor and material
  • Concrete and finish work: cutting and patching a slab adds time
  • Pump, pit and backup: a larger pump and a backup unit raise the total
  • Access and finished space: tight or finished basements slow the work

Insurers usually treat slow seepage differently from sudden water damage, and a policy may sell sewer backup coverage as an extra, so read your policy before you plan on a claim.
