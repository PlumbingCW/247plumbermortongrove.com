Wet basement floor guide

How to Waterproof a Basement Floor: What Morton Grove, IL Homes Need to Know

A coating alone rarely stops a wet basement floor. Learn which fixes manage water and which ones only hide it.

By the Morton Grove 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 15, 2026. Updated October 15, 2026.

Call (847) 281-8626

To waterproof a basement floor, you first have to decide whether you are blocking water or draining it. A paint-on coating blocks moisture vapor and small seepage, but it fails against real water pressure from the soil. Floors that stay wet after storms usually need interior drain tile and a sump pump, so the water has somewhere to go.

This guide walks through how to test your floor, which fixes suit which problem and what Morton Grove homeowners should check about floor drains and sewer backup before spending money on any coating.

Start by finding out where the water comes from

Water on a basement floor has only a few sources, and each one needs a different fix. Treating the wrong one wastes a weekend and a lot of product. Spend a day or two on diagnosis before you buy anything.

The cheapest test uses a square of plastic sheet. Tape an 18-inch piece flat to the bare concrete on all four sides with duct tape, leave it for 48 hours, then look. Moisture on the underside of the plastic means water vapor is rising through the slab. Moisture on top of the plastic means condensation from damp indoor air. Water pooling around the edges means liquid water is coming up at the joints.

Signs your floor needs more than a coating

  • Water appears at the wall and floor joint after heavy rain.
  • A dark, damp line follows cracks in the slab.
  • Puddles form near the floor drain or sump pit.
  • Efflorescence, a white powdery crust, builds up on the concrete.
  • Paint or old coatings bubble and peel off in sheets.
  • The basement smells musty even when the dehumidifier runs.

If the plastic test shows only light vapor, a quality masonry sealer and better ventilation may be enough. If water pools or the crack weeps, move on to drainage.

Hydrostatic pressure: why coatings let go

Soil around a foundation holds water after rain. That water presses on the slab from below and the walls from the side, and the force is called hydrostatic pressure. A thin coating cannot resist it. The water pushes through the weakest spot or lifts the coating off the concrete.

This is why a floor can look dry for one season and then fail after a spring storm. The coating did not leak, it was simply overpowered. Systems that last do not fight the pressure. They relieve it by giving water a path to a pit, and the pump moves it outside the house.

Notice that no single fix covers every row. A wet floor after a storm may have two causes at once, such as groundwater through a crack and sewage rising through a drain.

Cracks, and which ones matter

Hairline shrinkage cracks are normal in slabs and often stay dry. A crack that is wider than a credit card, has one side higher than the other, or leaks after rain deserves attention. Injecting a crack stops that point of entry, but if the water table is high, new cracks may appear nearby. A repair that does not address the water source is only a delay.

Interior drain tile and the sump pit

Interior drain tile is a perforated pipe set in gravel around the inside edge of the footing. A plumber or waterproofing crew cuts a channel in the slab, lays the pipe, and routes it to a sump pit. When water rises in the pit, the pump switches on and sends it out through a discharge line. The channel is then patched with new concrete.

It is a bigger job than a coating, but it manages water instead of masking it. The sump pump is the part that fails most often, so ask about a backup and a check valve on the discharge line. You can read more about options on our basement waterproofing page, and sump sizing and replacement are covered under sump pump installation and repair.

The floor drain: the weak spot many homeowners miss

A floor drain connects to the sewer. In a storm, when the sewer fills, water and waste can back up through that drain into the basement. Waterproofing the slab will not stop it. If the water on your floor smells like sewage, looks gray or black, or comes up through the drain, treat it as a sewer problem.

Avoid contact with the water, keep children and pets away, and do not run water in the house. Call a licensed plumber to check the line. A backwater valve or an overhead sewer conversion is the fix, and the cost depends on how your home is plumbed.

While you wait for help with a flooded basement

  • Do not walk through standing water if the electrical panel or outlets may be wet. Keep away from them.
  • Switch off the breaker only when the ground around the panel is dry and the switch is easy to reach.
  • Keep out of any water that smells like sewage. It carries bacteria.
  • Stop using sinks, tubs and washers so less water enters the drain.
  • Move belongings up off the floor if you can do it safely.

A practical order of work

If your basement floor is damp or occasionally wet, this sequence avoids wasted effort. Plenty of owners start with the coating and finish with a drain tile system two years later.

  1. Fix the outside first. Extend downspouts several feet from the foundation and grade soil away from the walls.
  2. Run the plastic sheet test for 48 hours to separate vapor from liquid water.
  3. Test the sump pump if you have one by pouring a bucket of water into the pit.
  4. Have a plumber check the floor drain and the lateral for backups.
  5. Choose between crack injection, a coating, or interior drainage based on what you found.
  6. Only then, apply a floor sealer or finish, because trapped moisture ruins most coatings.

What drives the price of waterproofing a basement floor

No honest price can be given without seeing the floor, and we do not list dollar figures here. These are the factors that move it.

What moves the cost of a basement floor fix

  • Linear feet of drain tile: A full perimeter costs far more than one wall, and the slab must be cut and patched along the route.
  • Sump pit and pump quality: Pump size, a battery backup and a sealed lid add to the total.
  • Discharge route: A long run to a safe outlet adds pipe and labor.
  • Floor drain and sewer work: A backwater valve or overhead sewer is a separate scope from drainage.
  • Finish after the work: New concrete, flooring and wall repair are extra trades.

Homeowners insurance usually excludes groundwater seepage and may cover sewer backup only with an endorsement. Read your policy before a storm, not after.
